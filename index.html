LEARN 1 • RECOGNISE

Why can a difficult call stay with us?

After a challenging encounter, the body and mind may remain in a heightened state even when the immediate situation has ended.

1
Body stays activated
Heart rate, muscle tension and alertness can remain elevated for a while.
2
Attention can stay on the call
You may replay events or find it harder to switch your attention to the next task.
3
Emotions can surface later
Upset, frustration, sadness or numbness may become noticeable after the immediate pressure has passed.
Learning point: noticing your response is not a sign of weakness. It is an opportunity to pause and choose what you need next.
LEARN 2 • MINDFULNESS

A short mindfulness pause

Mindfulness means deliberately bringing your attention to what is happening right now, rather than becoming caught up in thoughts about the previous call or the next job.

This short exercise uses your breathing and attention to create a transition between patient encounters.

How it works

Follow the dot with your finger. As it moves to the right, breathe in. As it returns, breathe out. Follow it at a comfortable pace — don't force or hold your breath. The dot is simply a guide to help you focus your attention.

LEARN 3 • RESET

Try the 60-second reset

This is a simple grounding sequence you can practise after a difficult job.

S
STOP
Pause for a moment. The call is over.
B
BREATHE
Take three slow breaths. Let your shoulders drop.
N
NOTICE
Notice what you are feeling physically and emotionally, without judging it.
R
RESET
Take a drink, loosen your hands, look around, and bring your attention back to the present.
Nice work.
The purpose is not to make difficult feelings disappear. It is to create a small pause so you can notice what you need.
LEARN 3 • REFLECT

Reflection: four useful questions

This section introduces a practical way of structuring reflection following a patient encounter, drawing on reflective practice and Neighbour's consultation model.

1
What happened?
Briefly describe the encounter and what stands out.
2
How did I feel?
Notice your emotional and physical response.
3
What can I learn?
Consider what went well, what was difficult, and what you might take forward.
4
What do I need now?
Identify any practical reset, conversation or support that would help.

LEARN 7 • SUPPORT

Knowing when to seek support

Sometimes a brief reset is enough. Sometimes a conversation or further support is appropriate.

Consider speaking to someone if…

  • the feelings are intense or continue after the immediate situation has passed;
  • you are struggling to concentrate or move on from the encounter;
  • you feel you need help before continuing your duties; or
  • you simply feel that talking to someone would help.
Support is part of professional self-care.
Use your organisation's approved clinical, operational, wellbeing or occupational-health pathways. In a real deployment, these links would be replaced with local Trust-approved routes.
